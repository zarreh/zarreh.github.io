## Why it exists

A care-management team can only call so many patients. Deciding who to call first is a ranking problem, and the most dangerous failure is a model that looks accurate on average while quietly under-serving one group.

Two things go wrong in practice. First, a score is often trained on a convenient label such as *future cost* when the real goal is *future need*. Groups that face barriers to care generate less cost for the same illness, so they are ranked as lower risk — the label-choice failure documented by Obermeyer et al. (*Science*, 2019). Second, a single overall accuracy figure hides the groups where the model performs badly.

The goal was a pipeline where both failures are measured and visible, and where no automated component can enrol a patient on its own.

Each step has a clear owner:

- **A calibrated model scores every patient.** A statistical model — not a chatbot — estimates each patient's chance of a high illness burden.
- **Fairness is audited per group.** Calibration and true-positive-rate parity are reported across age, sex, race and insurance strata with confidence intervals. Strata with too few patients show "insufficient data" instead of a misleading number.
- **An AI agent assembles the evidence.** For each flagged patient it gathers encounters, labs and care gaps into a short brief: drivers, corroborating evidence and what is missing. It cannot change the score or recommend enrolment.
- **A clinician decides.** Nothing happens to a patient without that decision.

### The label-choice experiment

The most instructive part of the app is a single interactive artifact: two otherwise identical models, one trained to predict cost and one trained to predict illness burden. On this synthetic cohort, which has a deliberately injected access gap, the cost-trained model enrols Black patients at a lower rate — a **5.7 percentage-point** gap between the two labels. It demonstrates the mechanism on controlled data; it is not a finding about any real health system.

## Results and honest limits

- **Gated in CI:** a validation harness fits both models on a frozen, race-stratified sample of the generated cohort and requires AUROC and calibration floors, correct small-sample handling, and that the label-choice gap never silently regresses to zero.
- **Scenario tests:** four hand-authored patients exercise the care-gap tools — including one with no recorded history, where every evidence category must be reported as missing.
- **Full-stack delivery:** a review queue of 19,409 flagged patients, case pages, the fairness audit and the label-choice chart, served by a persistent API and a Next.js interface covered by Playwright browser tests.

<Callout type="limit">
The LLM-backed evidence agent and brief writer are **not** covered by the automated gate; the graph's control flow around them is tested with stand-ins. The data is synthetic, and the 5.7-point gap comes from an access gap injected on purpose — it illustrates a mechanism rather than measuring one.
</Callout>

## Engineering decisions

- **Model and agent are separated by contract.** The risk pipeline — features, calibration, thresholds, fairness audit — may not import any LLM framework, and an import-linter rule enforces it in CI.
- **Typed tools, not text-to-SQL.** The agent can only call a fixed set of read-only evidence tools, so its reach into patient data is bounded and testable.
- **Human review is a real graph interrupt.** The case flow suspends at the decision step and resumes through the API once a clinician decides, with a streaming trace of each step.
- **No fabricated numbers.** Below the minimum sample size, the audit reports a subgroup as missing rather than computing a statistic from a handful of patients.

## Who it's for

For **health systems and payers**, it shows how to put a risk model, a fairness audit and a human reviewer into one auditable workflow. For **teams evaluating AI in regulated settings**, it shows how to keep an LLM useful while keeping it out of the decision path.

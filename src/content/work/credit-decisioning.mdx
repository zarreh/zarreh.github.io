## Why it exists

In the United States, ECOA and Regulation B require a written notice with the principal reasons when credit is declined. "Insufficient score" is explicitly not enough, and the CFPB has said the duty is the same when a complex model makes the decision. In practice, two things go wrong:

- **Reasons drift from what the model used** — after a template edit, a mapping change or a reviewer's rewording.
- **Generated text adds things nobody checked** — a wrong number, a promise, or a prohibited basis such as age or sex.

The applicant cannot contest a reason they were never given, and the lender carries the regulatory exposure. The project asks a practical question: *how do you prove a regulated document is correct before it is sent?*

Every artifact has a producer and a separate check:

| Artifact | Produced by | Checked by |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Probability of a bad outcome | Calibrated logistic regression | Repeated 5×5 cross-validation, bootstrap intervals, reliability curve |
| Approve / refer / decline band | Code, from the benchmark's cost matrix | Unit tests pin the derived (not tuned) threshold |
| Principal reasons | Code, from the model's coefficients | Attributions must sum to the model's log-odds difference |
| Notice text | Code, from a reviewed template | Eight deterministic verifier checks |
| Analyst answers | A read-only LLM agent | Code checks every number against tool results |

A reviewer sees the model's reasons, the verifier's results and the drafted notice, then records the decision. The model's recommendation stays on record even when the human overrides it.

### The verifier is itself verified

The verifier is a second, independent implementation — it never calls the renderer. It checks that reasons match the derivation, that there are one to four of them, that none rests on a prohibited basis, that each states the applicant's actual value, that the decision is consistent, that every number matches the application, that the legal text matches a pinned hash, and that each sentence is approved wording.

To prove those checks earn their place, a **mutation suite** applies 14 kinds of defect to every genuine notice and requires each one to fail its *intended* check. A meta-test then switches each of the eight checks off in turn and requires the gate to notice.

## Results and honest limits

All figures come from one generated run (2026-10-29) on the benchmark's 1,000 applicants with out-of-fold predictions.

- **Decision quality:** expected cost of 0.541 per applicant (95% interval 0.488–0.598) against 0.70 for declining everyone — the right baseline, because declining a good applicant costs 1 and approving a bad one costs 5. AUROC is 0.780 (0.751–0.810).
- **Model choice:** a boosted-tree challenger gained +0.012 AUROC with an interval that includes zero, so the simpler, explainable model was kept.
- **Verifier:** every applicable mutant was caught across 1,006 genuine notices, no genuine notice was rejected, and all eight checks were detected when disabled.
- **Analyst agent:** 19 of 19 scripted guardrail cases pass. A separate live run of 15 questions behaved correctly on all 15 after fixes, with one answer flagged for a real transcription error — a smoke test, not a rate.

<Callout type="limit">
Protected attributes are excluded from the model, yet applicants under 25 are approved at **0.57** of the rate of the reference group — below the common 0.8 screen. Dropping a column does not remove its signal. Small groups are labelled "insufficient n" rather than given a number, and nothing here generalizes from 1,000 German applicants in the 1970s–90s to a current lender.
</Callout>

<Callout type="lesson" title="A lesson worth sharing">
The first version let an LLM paraphrase each reason. Prose cannot be fully verified by code, so the notice became 100% template and the LLM moved to a read-only analyst role, where its numbers *can* be checked. Taking the model out of the notice made the compliance claim stronger, not weaker. Evaluation also caught a real bug in the analyst's number-checking guard, which is now pinned by tests.
</Callout>

## Engineering decisions

- **No LLM on the decision path**, enforced by an import contract rather than by convention.
- **The review workflow is a plain state machine** with atomic claims — a double start or a stale page returns a conflict — and a verified-hash binding before a notice can be sent.
- **Reproducible by construction:** pinned, hash-checked data, and a single command that retrains from scratch and requires the evidence to match.
- **Quality gates on every pull request:** lint, strict typing, import contracts, 90%+ test coverage and an evaluation gate.

## Who it's for

For **lenders and fintech teams**, it shows an approach to regulated, explainable decisions where every outbound document is independently verified. For **AI and risk leaders**, it is a worked example of using mutation testing to prove that your safety checks actually catch failures.

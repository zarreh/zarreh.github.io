## Why it exists

Usage-based insurance prices risk from behavior rather than from proxies such as age or postcode. That requires two things traditional rating models do not handle well: turning noisy, high-frequency sensor data into meaningful driving features, and predicting both *whether* a claim will happen and *how expensive* it is likely to be.

- **Feature engineering** converts trip data into more than 50 features: harsh-driving intensity, speeding patterns, distance, night driving, time-of-day patterns and a composite risk score.
- **Two models, two questions.** A classifier predicts claim probability; a separate regressor predicts claim amount. Class imbalance is handled with `scale_pos_weight`, and claim amounts are log-transformed.
- **Tuned with Optuna**, then versioned in a simple model registry for batch or single-trip inference.
- **Interpretable output:** each driver gets a risk tier and the factors behind it.

## Results and honest limits

From the project's experiments on a 1,200-driver dataset:

- **Claim probability:** ROC-AUC improved from 0.524 to 0.689 and recall doubled after tuning and feature work.
- **Claim severity:** RMSE fell from $2,156 to $1,847 and R² rose from 0.398 to 0.485.
- **Top predictive features:** composite risk score, harsh-driving intensity, speed risk, total distance and night driving.

<Callout type="limit">
Claims are rare, and it shows: even after tuning, F1 is about 0.06 and precision about 0.08, so the classifier is a ranking signal for pricing and coaching, not a reliable predictor of individual claims. The dataset is small, and results would need validation on a real insurer's portfolio before informing any pricing decision.
</Callout>

## Engineering decisions

- **Separate frequency and severity models**, mirroring how actuaries decompose expected loss.
- **Explicit imbalance handling** and log-transformed targets rather than accepting a model that predicts "no claim" for everyone.
- **Reproducible environment:** Docker and Docker Compose for training, inference and notebooks, with command-line, Python and notebook interfaces.

## Who it's for

For **insurers exploring usage-based products**, it is a transparent starting point that separates claim frequency from severity. For **data scientists**, it is an honest example of what imbalanced claim data does to headline metrics.

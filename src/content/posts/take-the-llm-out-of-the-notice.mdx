When a lender declines an application in the United States, the applicant is owed a written notice with the principal reasons. "Insufficient score" does not count, and regulators have been clear that the obligation is the same when a complex model makes the decision. That makes the adverse-action notice a useful test case for a broader question: **how do you prove that a document produced with AI is correct before anyone sends it?**

I built a [credit-decisioning prototype](/work/credit-decisioning/) to find out. The most important design decision turned out to be the one I reversed.

## The first version let the model write

The obvious architecture looks like this: a calibrated model scores the application, a policy turns the score into approve, refer or decline, and an LLM turns the model's top reasons into a clear, friendly paragraph. It reads well, and it demos beautifully.

It is also nearly impossible to verify.

Prose has too many degrees of freedom. A paraphrase can soften a reason until it no longer matches what the model used. It can add a number that is slightly wrong, a reassurance that sounds like a promise, or — the one that should keep compliance teams up at night — a phrase that hints at a prohibited basis such as age. You can ask another model to check the first one, but then you have two probabilistic systems vouching for each other.

## The second version let code write — and an independent verifier check

So the notice became 100% template. Code derives the reasons from the model's coefficients, code fills a reviewed template, and a **separate verifier** — written independently, never calling the renderer — checks every notice before a human sees it:

- the reasons match the derivation, and there are between one and four of them;
- none rests on a prohibited basis;
- each reason states the applicant's actual value;
- the decision is consistent with the band;
- every number matches the application;
- the legal text matches a pinned hash;
- every sentence is approved wording.

That last property is only possible because the text is no longer free-form. Deterministic output can be checked deterministically.

## Then verify the verifier

A verifier that never fails is not evidence of anything. To show that each check earns its place, the project runs a **mutation suite**: it applies 14 kinds of deliberate defect to every genuine notice — swap a reason, change a number, inject a prohibited phrase — and requires each mutant to fail the *specific* check designed to catch it. A meta-test then switches each of the eight checks off in turn and requires the overall gate to notice.

On the benchmark run, every applicable mutant was caught across 1,006 genuine notices, and no genuine notice was rejected. That is the kind of sentence I want to be able to say to a risk committee: not "the model is very good", but "here is the evidence that our safety checks catch the failures we care about".

## The LLM did not disappear — it moved

Taking the model out of the notice did not mean taking it out of the product. It moved into a **read-only analyst** role: a reviewer can ask it to explain a case, compare applicants or summarize the drivers of a decision. In that role, the model's claims *can* be checked — code compares every number it states against the tool results it retrieved.

Evaluation of that analyst also caught a real bug in its number-checking guard, which is now pinned by tests. That is the point of building the checks: they find things.

## What this means beyond credit

The pattern generalizes to any regulated or high-stakes output — clinical summaries, claims decisions, compliance findings:

1. **Decide what must be provably correct.** For those artifacts, prefer templates and code over generated prose.
2. **Put the model where its output can be checked.** Explanation, retrieval and drafting for a human are good fits; final regulated text usually is not.
3. **Build verifiers independently of generators,** and prove them with mutation testing rather than trusting a green dashboard.
4. **Keep humans accountable for the decision,** and keep the model's recommendation on record even when they override it.

Moving the model out of the notice made the compliance claim stronger, not weaker. In my experience, that trade — a little less fluency for a lot more evidence — is almost always the right one when the stakes are real.
